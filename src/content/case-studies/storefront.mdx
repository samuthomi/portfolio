## The situation

A year after launching Sendy Fulfillment, sellers had adopted warehouse storage because it cut delivery times and simplified their operations. That adoption created the next opportunity: help sellers move from stored inventory to completed sales without extra manual work.

The concept was a self-checkout storefront. Sellers publish a product page from their warehouse inventory, customers buy directly and Sendy handles delivery.

## What was actually at risk

The design work was the smaller part. The bigger risk was building something that didn't match how sellers win customers' trust, set prices and get paid.

Before we could design anything, we needed to answer four questions: would sellers trust Sendy to collect payments on their behalf? What payment options were non-negotiable? What needed to be configurable for sellers to run a real business through this? And what was the smallest version that would still work for real sellers?

## How we validated

Because this was a new product concept with no existing user behaviour to learn from, I pushed for a demo-first approach: prototype quickly, test with real sellers and let their feedback set the backlog.

**First round (5 sellers):** I built a rough mobile prototype and tested the core flow. Setup and checkout felt intuitive, but sellers wanted fewer steps to get live. They were excited about Sendy collecting payments. Some wanted to customise the branding.

What changed: we tightened the flow so sellers publish first and configure later, and anchored the product around payment collection as the core value.

<Figure src="/images/storefront-manager.webp" width={1100} height={1040} alt="The Storefront tab in the seller app: a live storefront link with Open and Share buttons, options to share a product link or a shopping cart link, and customisation, offers and store performance marked as coming soon." caption="The Storefront tab in the seller app. The link is live and ready to share, while customisation is marked as coming soon." />

**Second round (20 sellers):** With more confidence in the direction, we tested a higher-fidelity prototype to shape the first release. This round raised harder findings: Payment on Delivery (cash or mobile money) was essential for customer trust. Sellers wanted to pass fulfilment fees to customers, but pricing configuration would be expensive to build. Sellers relied on discounts and bundles to make sales.

## Key decisions

<PullQuote>

Most of what followed were product decisions about scope and trust.

</PullQuote>

**Get sellers live first.** Reduce steps. Get sellers to a shareable storefront fast. Configuration could come later.

**Build in the payment option customers trust.** Sellers told us they would not adopt the product without Payment on Delivery, so it moved out of phase two.

**Keep essential features apart from expensive ones.** Pricing configuration was important, but we were open about the build cost and planned it for later rather than squeezing it into the first release.

**Plan for how sellers make sales.** Sellers use discounts and bundles to drive revenue, so a storefront without them would not fit how they sell. Offers and discounts needed platform-wide backend support from other teams, so they stayed high priority but blocked. The Storefront tab shows them as coming soon.

## What shipped

The final design included a desktop and mobile storefront where customers could browse and order, and a new Storefront tab inside the Sendy Fulfillment app for sellers to manage their catalogue, customise their page and share links.

**Shared carts.** Sellers were already taking orders from buyers over chat and closing them by hand. We gave them a way to do it faster: build the cart, send the customer a checkout link, and let the buyer complete the order. The buyer keeps full control of the cart items during checkout.

## What didn't ship

The product was handed off for development but never released. Layoffs and changing business priorities at Sendy ended the project before launch.

<PullQuote>

Even without a release, the work validated what a storefront needed to work in this market: payment options customers trust, a light setup, and pricing sellers control.

</PullQuote>

## Reflection

Involving sellers early and often kept us focused on problems that mattered to them. Without them, we would have built something that looked good and that sellers would not have used.
